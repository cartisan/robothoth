## Summary
* Quantifies the statistical relationship between two random variables
* Calculated as the [[Covariance - a measure of the joint variability of two variables|covariance]], normalized by the product of the two variables standard deviations to yield a scale-invariant metric
* In ML, it is critical for feature selection and identifying multicollinearity, as it reveals the degree of linear dependence between input features or between a feature and the target variable.
* The coefficient ranges strictly from $-1$ to $+1$, where extrema represent perfect linear alignment and $0$ implies no linear association.
* **To validate a correlation, check significance!** A high correlation with a high p-value ($>0.05$) suggests the relationship could be random noise.
* Computation:

$$r = \frac{\text{cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

- $\text{cov}(X,Y)$: 
	- Sum of the product of deviations from the mean for each data point.
	- If $X$ and $Y$ are both above/below their means simultaneously, this sum is positive.
- $\sigma_X \sigma_Y$: 
	- The product of the "spread" of $X$ and $Y$. 
	- This acts as a scaling factor, forcing the result to stay within the $[-1, 1]$ boundary.
## Pitfalls
1. The "Anscombe's Quartet" Problem
	- Summary statistics can be deceptive: four totally different datasets can share nearly identical statistical properties (mean, variance, and correlation of $r \approx 0.816$), yet represent entirely different relationships.
	- Never rely on $r$ alone,  **always visualize the data**![[Pasted image 20251120111219.png|400]]
2. Correlation $\neq$ Causation (Spurious Correlations)
	- A high correlation can exists between two variables that have no causal link, due to a third **confounding variable**. 
	- In ML, these features can lead to models that fail in production when the confounder shifts (covariate shift)
3. The "Zero-Correlation" Trap (Non-Linearity)
	- Pearson correlation only measures **linear** relationships. 
		- A correlation of $r = 0$ does **not** mean the variables are independent; it means they are not linearly related.
		- A perfect parabolic relationship ($Y = X^2$) centered at 0 will have a Pearson correlation of $0$.
	- If $r$ is low, check for non-linear patterns using Mutual Information or Spearman’s Rank correlation.
4. Sensitivity to Outliers
	* Pearson's $r$ is not robust, a single extreme outlier in a small dataset can heavily affect it.
	* **Check for outliers** using Z-scores or Interquartile Range (IQR) 
		* Consider using robust correlation methods (like Kendall’s Tau) if the data is noisy
## Related Measures
| Metric                  | Symbol         | Range               | Physical Unit                               | Answers the Question                                           |
| ----------------------- | -------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Correlation             | $r$ or $\rho$  | $[-1, 1]$           | Unitless (Scale Invariant)                  | How tightly and in what direction are these variables coupled? |
| Coeff. of Determination | $R^2$          | $[0, 1]$            | Unitless (Percentage)                       | How much of the variation in $Y$ is explained by $X$?          |
| Slope                   | $m$ or $\beta$ | $(-\infty, \infty)$ | $\frac{\Delta Y_{units}}{\Delta X_{units}}$ | For every unit increase in $X$, how much does $Y$ change?      
1. Correlation ($r$): The "Tightness" of the Fit
	- Measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship.
	- Key nuance
		- It is *normalized*, a correlation of $0.99$ means the data points fall almost perfectly on a line, but it does not tell you if that line is steep or flat.
	- Use case:
		- Feature selection (removing redundant features that provide the same information).
2. [[R² -Coefficient of Determination|Coefficient of Determination]] ($R^2$): The "Explanatory Power"
	- The proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variable.
	- Key nuance:
		- Unlike $r$, $R^2$ *does not indicate direction* , it focuses strictly on goodness-of-fit.
	- Use case:
		- Model evaluation (benchmarking overall performance).
3. Slope ($\beta$): The "Steepness" of the Regression Line
	- The rate of change of the dependent variable with respect to the independent variable.
	+ Key nuance:
		+ It is *sensitive to scale*, if you change the units of your predictor from meters to kilometers, the slope will multiply by 1000, but the correlation ($r$) will remain exactly the same.
	+ Use case:
		+ Model interpretation (understanding the impact/ROI of a specific feature).
